Explosion Risks in Industrial Silos

Dust explosions in silos are among the most destructive industrial accidents.

What Is a Dust Explosion?

A dust explosion occurs when five factors are present: combustible dust, oxygen, an ignition source, dust concentration within the explosive range, and confinement. Nearly all organic materials and many metals can explode as dust. The lower explosive limit typically falls between 20 and 60 g/m³.

Ignition Sources in Silos

Potential ignition sources include mechanical sparks from tools, welding and cutting, static electricity, self-heating in the material, and hot equipment surfaces. All these sources must be controlled through systematic maintenance and procedures.

The ATEX Directive and Explosion Zoning

In the EU, explosive atmospheres are regulated by the ATEX directives 2014/34/EU and 1999/92/EC. Equipment must be certified for the correct zone.

Zone 20

Continuous explosive atmosphere — inside silos.

Zone 21

Periodic explosive atmosphere.

Zone 22

Rare explosive atmosphere.

Preventing Dust Explosions

Prevention focuses on removing one or more sides of the explosion pentagon: reducing dust concentration through good housekeeping, inerting with nitrogen or CO2, explosion vents for pressure relief, and maintaining grounding against static discharge.

Historical Dust Explosions

Some of the most notable dust explosions include the Imperial Sugar factory in Georgia in 2008, with 14 killed; the Washburn A Mill in Minneapolis in 1878, with 18 killed; and the CTA explosion in Blaye, France in 1997, with 11 killed. All of these accidents could have been prevented with better maintenance and housekeeping.
